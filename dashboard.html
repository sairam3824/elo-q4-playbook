01 Network identity

This is not an instalment network.

Parcelado — splitting a purchase across monthly instalments — is the defining behaviour of Brazilian credit cards. On this ledger it's almost absent. Elo is a high‑frequency, small‑ticket, everyday‑spend network: the card for lunch, fuel, groceries and the pharmacy, not the one used to finance a television.

02 Who transacts on our cards

A steep Pareto, and four cardholders — not one.

A promotions programme run as a broadcast spends most of its budget on cardholders who contribute almost nothing, while under-serving the group that funds the network. Segmenting on value per active month and merchant breadth — the two levers a promotions team can actually pull — splits the base into four distinct cardholders.

04 Growth

The network grows by adding cards, not by deepening them.

Transactions roughly double across the window, but transactions per active card barely move. That is the series a promotions programme, whose whole job is the second thing, should show up in — and currently doesn't.

18.2 → 24.8 → 19.5 · flat, not deepening

Active cards climb steadily across the window — 241 to 398. Transactions per active card barely move, bumping up only for a December seasonal peak (avg ticket follows the same shape: R$81.49 → R$98.19 → R$88.17) before settling back. The growth line is cards, not usage per card.

08 The Q4 targeting map

Where to point the promotions programme.

Every merchant subsector scored on two indices. Novelty Capture Index (NCI) — new-merchant volume share ÷ incumbent volume share: above 1, cardholders come here to try something new. Coverage Index (COV) — merchant supply share ÷ incumbent volume share: above 1, more supply than demand justifies.

Acquisition engine (NCI ≥ 1.2) Habit anchor, under-supplied Over-served — promotion waste (COV ≥ 1.2) Bubble size = share of incumbent transactions
The workhorse

Subsector 37

18.8% of volume — the single largest subsector. R$49 avg ticket. NCI 1.12 is just below the discovery threshold: defend top-of-wallet, don't chase acquisition.

Habit anchor

Subsector 33

18.6% of volume, R$69 avg ticket. NCI 0.42, COV 0.50 — cardholders go where they already go, and merchant supply can't keep up. Recruit, don't discount.

Acquisition engine

Subsector 27

4.4% of incumbent volume → 9.6% of novelty volume. R$175 avg ticket, NCI 2.16, COV 3.49. The clearest case for a discovery offer.

Over-served

Subsector 41

0.7% of volume, R$110 avg ticket. NCI 0.96, COV 1.33 — more merchant supply than demand or discovery justifies. Promotion spend here is waste by construction.

Acquisition engines also include subsectors 18, 20, 32, 36, 12, 30, 25, 31, 21, 17 and 1. Habit anchors needing merchant recruitment also include 29, 19, 34, 16, 7, 8, 15 and 39 — together with 33 and 37, the bottom-left group is the majority of network volume.

09 Recommendations for Q4

Six moves, in priority order.

01

Stop discounting habit. Redirect that budget to discovery.

Subsectors 33, 29, 19 and 34 are 50.8% of transactions with novelty capture below 0.9 — discounts here subsidise purchases that would have happened anyway. Move a third of that budget to the acquisition-engine quadrant (27, 18, 20, 32, 36).

02

Promote down the pyramid, not up it.

Tier A is 0.3% of merchants and 26% of incumbent volume, but only 17% of new-merchant volume. Reserve a defined share of Q4 slots for accelerating Tier C and D merchants, where new relationships actually form.

03

Fix the category_1 = Y decline problem before spending.

That flag carries 4.3× the odds of a decline across 6.1% of volume. Promoting into a population the network then turns away costs money twice — a one-week question for risk, before the calendar locks.

04

Use instalments in exactly one place: the first visit.

Instalment use nearly doubles at new merchants. An instalment-linked offer works with the network's nature at a first visit, and against it at a known merchant — the one instalment play the data supports.

05

Recruit merchants where demand outruns supply.

State 9 (39% of demand, 28% of supply) and subsector 33 (coverage 0.50) are merchant-acquisition problems wearing a promotions costume. State 22 is the cleanest greenfield in the network.

06

Segment the send.

Core Everyday (72% of spend) needs retention, not discounts. Big-Ticket Narrow needs reactivation. Explorer Light is the natural discovery audience. Occasional — a third of cards, 8.7% of spend — is the last call on the budget, not the first.

What this analysis cannot tell you

Stating the limits is part of the answer.

  • No card-level loyalty file. Tenure and value are rebuilt from observed behaviour; the business's own loyalty score can't be explained from this export.
  • The two windows are different card samples — only 16 cards in common. Findings support where discovery happens, not that a promotion caused a given card to switch.
  • 463 cards is a small panel. Portfolio-level conclusions are stable; state×subsector cells with a few hundred transactions are indicative only.
  • Everything is anonymised. Someone inside Elo has to put real names on the subsectors before this becomes a media plan.
  • The currency transform is empirically recovered, not supplied — validated by distribution shape, but worth confirming against one known settlement record.
  • Correlation, not causation. The targeting map ranks where discovery already happens; proving a promotion causes it needs a holdout test.
Source data

Three files: a 463-card historical ledger, a 7,527-card new-merchant window (99,900 transactions, 16 cards in common with the historical panel), and a 334,633-row merchant reference table.

Currency recovery

The normalised purchase_amount field is an affine transform of real Reais:

BRL = purchase_amount ÷ 0.00150265 + 497.06
Outlier handling

Tickets above R$4,000 (99 records, 0.1% of rows, likely settlement or reversal artefacts) are excluded from every value statistic. Mean ticket falls from R$272.11 to R$88.24 once removed — the difference between a mid-ticket story and the true one.
